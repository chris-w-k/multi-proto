What it takes

Voice output is nearly free. Voice input is the entire problem, and it changes the pedagogy — not just the interface.

Why only one of them talks

An earlier version had both characters speaking, the tutor glossing each phrase aloud as the shopkeeper said it. It sounds supportive and it is actually the opposite: the learner never has to reach for anything, because the answer arrives unprompted half a second later. Immersion with a running translation is not immersion.

So Deniz speaks the target language and nothing else, and Pip stays quiet until summoned. Support moves from something pushed at the learner to something they pull — one word at a time, by tapping it. That single change makes help measurable: every tap is a recorded admission of "I didn't have this one", which is a far better signal than anything you can infer from a learner who was told everything anyway.

The bubble is the interface

Deniz's words appear as he says them and stay put through your turn, so you can study the sentence you are answering rather than holding it in your head. Words you have never met are bold; once seen, they settle into normal weight. Over a session the bubble visibly de-bolds — progress you can see without a progress bar.

Free

Speech synthesis. Built into every browser, no network, no cost, per-character voice and language, adjustable rate — so "speech speed" from the PRD's difficulty model becomes a real dial rather than a listed dimension.

Hard

Recognition. Needs mic permission, a real ASR service in production, and a confidence policy. Browser speech recognition is Chrome/Safari-only and blocked in most embedded contexts.

Dangerous

Grading on the transcript. The recogniser becomes the examiner, and it is worse at accented child speech than at anything else it hears.

The failure mode to design against first

Run language detection over real transcripts of children struggling in a second language and you see what recognisers do with them: unintelligible audio comes back as short fragments in scripts nobody in the room speaks. That is harmless in a chat log. In a system that scores production, it is a child being marked wrong for having an accent — the precise opposite of what this feature is for.

So the rule this prototype implements: low recogniser confidence is never a wrong answer. Below the threshold the turn is not graded and Deniz simply asks again. The matcher also checks every alternative the recogniser offers, not just the top one. Both cost nothing and they are the difference between an encouraging tutor and a discouraging one.

What voice adds that text can't measure

  • Time before speaking. The silence between Deniz finishing and the child starting is the closest thing you have to a confidence measure. It should drive the scaffolding, and it is in the metrics panel.
  • Whether they speak at all. A child who types a word and a child who says it out loud are not doing the same thing. Spoken-vs-typed is the headline voice metric.
  • Which words they reach for help on. Taps are per-word and timestamped — a vocabulary difficulty ranking that falls out of normal use.

Turn-taking is where voice tutors die

Measure any production voice tutor and the same two faults cluster around moments of difficulty: agent turns truncated mid-sentence, and the agent repeating itself. Both are turn-taking failures, and beginners provoke them — they pause mid-sentence to think, the endpointer reads the pause as a finished turn, the agent talks over them, they stop.

Silence tolerance should be a function of support level — three or four seconds early on, one and a half once they are fluent in the scenario. That single parameter is probably worth more to a beginner than any amount of content work.

Roughly a month of one engineer plus design, on top of an existing realtime voice stack. The content — beats, accepted answers, the phrase dictionary — is the long pole, and it is the part an LLM should be generating offline into a reviewable format rather than improvising live.

What this page is not

Still a scripted engine: authored beats, no model at runtime. Voice here is the browser's own synthesis and recognition, which is why it may refuse to listen inside an embedded frame — the banner at the top says which parts your browser granted. In production none of this is the browser's job; it is the realtime agent's, and everything above should be read as a spec for that, not as an implementation.
