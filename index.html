What it takes

Voice output is nearly free. Voice input is the entire problem, and it changes the pedagogy — not just the interface.

The idea worth keeping

In text, colour tells the learner which language they are hearing. In audio there is no colour, and a single voice sliding between two languages is genuinely confusing for a beginner. Two characters solve it. Deniz only ever speaks the target language; Pip only ever speaks the learner's. The child never has to work out which language a sound belongs to — they know by who said it.

That turns the abstract scaffolding ladder into something a five-year-old can feel. Level 0 is Pip narrating with Deniz saying one word. Level 5 is Pip silent, still on screen, still available. Progress becomes a character receding, not a number going up — and "Pip hasn't spoken for three turns" is a far better felt reward than a scaffolding percentage. The rail's talk-time split is that same ladder measured honestly.

Free

Speech synthesis. Built into every browser, no network, no cost, per-character voice and language, adjustable rate — so "speech speed" from the PRD's difficulty model becomes a real dial rather than a listed dimension.

Hard

Recognition. Needs mic permission, a real ASR service in production, and a confidence policy. Browser speech recognition is Chrome/Safari-only and blocked in most embedded contexts — including, possibly, the frame this page is in.

Dangerous

Grading on the transcript. The recogniser becomes the examiner, and it is worse at accented child speech than at anything else it hears.

The failure mode to design against first

Run language detection over real transcripts of children struggling in a second language and you see what recognisers do with them: unintelligible audio comes back as short fragments in scripts nobody in the room speaks. That is harmless in a chat log. In a system that scores production, it is a child being marked wrong for having an accent — the precise opposite of what this feature is for.

So the rule this prototype implements: low recogniser confidence is never a wrong answer. Below the threshold, Pip asks again and the turn is not graded. It also checks every alternative the recogniser offers, not just the top one — if any of them match, the learner said it. Both behaviours cost nothing and they are the difference between an encouraging tutor and a discouraging one.

What voice adds that text can't measure

  • Time before speaking. The silence between Deniz finishing and the child starting is the closest thing you have to a confidence measure. It should drive the scaffolding, and it is in the rail.
  • Whether they speak at all. A child who types "kahve" and a child who says it out loud are not doing the same thing. Spoken-vs-typed is the headline voice metric.
  • Pronunciation, sideways. The PRD excludes pronunciation assessment from MVP, correctly. But confidence scores give you a free, badly-biased proxy — useful for spotting words the recogniser systematically fails on, never for telling a child they said it wrong.

Turn-taking is where voice tutors die

Measure any production voice tutor and the same two faults cluster around moments of difficulty: agent turns truncated mid-sentence, and the agent repeating itself. Both are turn-taking failures, and beginners provoke them — they pause mid-sentence to think, the endpointer reads the pause as a finished turn, the agent talks over them, they stop.

Silence tolerance should be a function of scaffolding level — three or four seconds at level 1, one and a half by level 5. That single parameter is probably worth more to a beginner than any amount of content work.

Roughly a month of one engineer plus design, on top of an existing realtime voice stack. The content — beats, accepted answers, repairs — is the long pole, and it is the part an LLM should be generating offline into a reviewable format rather than improvising live.

What this page is not

Still a scripted engine: authored beats, no model at runtime. Voice here is the browser's own synthesis and recognition, which is why it may refuse to listen inside an embedded frame — the banner at the top says which parts your browser granted. In production none of this is the browser's job; it is the realtime agent's, and everything above should be read as a spec for that, not as an implementation.
